> *Originally written for and submitted to Bridge Michigan, this op-ed ultimately went unpublished. I'm sharing it here as part of my independent writing.*

Michigan is rolling out the welcome mat for some of the largest data center projects in the country.

Not everyone is sure what to make of the new arrival.

The proposed [Stargate campus in Saline Township](https://www.latitudemedia.com/news/what-the-michigan-stargate-site-says-about-todays-ai-market/) and Google's [Project Cannoli in Van Buren Township](https://www.panattoni.com/vanburen/) represent billions of dollars in investment and signal Michigan's ambition to compete in the rapidly growing artificial intelligence economy.

Supporters make a compelling case. In a recent [Bridge Michigan guest commentary](https://bridgemi.com/guest-commentary/opinion-building-ai-infrastructure-the-michigan-way/), OpenAI Chief Global Affairs Officer Chris Lehane argued that building AI infrastructure presents a significant economic opportunity for the state.

Yet many residents are asking a different question:

> **What does Michigan get in return?**

Across town halls, community meetings, and online discussions, concerns tend to sound remarkably similar. Residents see large developments consuming farmland, reshaping local landscapes, increasing demand on infrastructure, and placing new pressure on the electrical grid. Meanwhile, the promised benefits often arrive in the form of statistics about investment, tax revenue, and future economic growth.

Those benefits may be real. But they can also feel distant compared to the changes occurring directly outside someone's front door.

The answer is not to reject these projects.

**The question is not whether Michigan should participate. The question is how Michigan can participate on the most advantageous terms possible.**

## AI Is Coming. Michigan Should Compete for It.

Whether artificial intelligence fulfills its most ambitious promises or falls short of them, one reality is already clear.

**The race to build AI infrastructure is underway.**

Technology companies, investors, utilities, and governments around the world are committing hundreds of billions of dollars to the data centers, power systems, and digital infrastructure required to support advanced computing. Analysts at [McKinsey & Company](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-cost-of-compute-a-7-trillion-dollar-race-to-scale-data-centers-for-ai) estimate that AI-related data center investment could reach trillions of dollars globally over the coming years.

Michigan will not determine whether that transformation occurs.

The question is whether Michigan chooses to participate in it.

If the next generation of digital infrastructure is going to be built somewhere, there is a strong argument that some of it should be built here. The state has a skilled workforce, available land, strong utility partners, world-class universities, and a long history of supporting transformative industries.

Michigan should absolutely compete for these investments.

But competing for investment does not mean accepting whatever terms happen to be offered.

## Communities Bring Value Too

Too often, discussions about economic development treat communities as passive beneficiaries.

They are not.

Communities contribute land, utility capacity, transportation infrastructure, public services, workforce development, and perhaps most importantly, the social license necessary for major projects to move forward.

**Those contributions have value.**

Michigan is not merely competing for investment. It is bringing assets to the negotiating table.

**Michigan should approach these opportunities with confidence and a clear understanding of its own value.**

The goal should not be to create barriers to investment. Nor should it be to demand symbolic concessions. The goal should be to identify opportunities where the interests of communities, utilities, residents, and developers align.

The most successful negotiations are not zero-sum. They create durable value for everyone involved.

In fact, communities across the country have already used [Community Benefit Agreements](https://climate.law.columbia.edu/content/community-benefits-agreements-database) to align major infrastructure and development projects with local priorities. These agreements demonstrate that economic development and community interests do not have to be competing objectives.

## Thinking Bigger Than Tax Revenue

Public reporting suggests that projects such as Stargate and Project Cannoli could eventually require power measured in gigawatts. A recent [WKAR analysis](https://www.wkar.org/michigans-data-center-divide/2026-02-27/michigan-data-centers-could-require-power-equal-to-six-nuclear-plants) noted that the proposed developments could require electricity comparable to multiple large power plants. Likewise, the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai) has identified AI and data centers as a rapidly growing source of global electricity demand.

Facilities operating at that scale are not simply commercial developments.

They become part of Michigan's long-term infrastructure landscape.

That creates an opportunity to think beyond traditional economic development incentives.

What if a project included community-scale battery storage that strengthened local grid resilience?

What if infrastructure investments helped keep hospitals, emergency services, and schools operating during major outages?

What if upgrades to the electrical system lowered costs or improved reliability for surrounding communities?

What if workforce development partnerships created pipelines into technical careers for Michigan students?

What if infrastructure improvements built to support data centers also strengthened the communities around them for decades to come?

These ideas are not merely theoretical. Community benefit agreements elsewhere have secured investments in workforce development, broadband access, education, and other community priorities through creative partnerships between developers and host communities. The [Greenlining Institute](https://greenlining.org/2022/community-benefit-agreement/) has documented numerous examples of this approach in practice.

With enough creativity, the conversation becomes far more interesting than jobs versus opposition.

Instead, it becomes a discussion about how Michigan can use AI investment to build stronger communities.

## The Michigan Way

Michigan has faced similar moments before.

The state did not become the center of the automotive industry simply because companies built factories here. Michigan invested in roads, utilities, manufacturing capacity, workforce development, and the infrastructure necessary to support long-term growth.

The result was not merely industrial success.

It was the creation of entire communities, industries, and generations of economic opportunity.

The AI economy presents a similar challenge.

> **The question is not whether AI comes to Michigan.**
> 
> **The question is what Michigan asks for when it arrives.**

Michigan should welcome investment. It should compete aggressively for jobs, infrastructure, and technological leadership.

But it should also recognize the value it brings to the table and negotiate accordingly.

The strongest economic development strategies do more than attract capital.

They ensure that growth leaves the host community stronger than it found it.

Michigan has an opportunity not only to host the infrastructure powering the next generation of technology, but also to demonstrate how that growth can be shared.

**Michigan is rolling out the welcome mat for data centers.**

**We should make sure we're negotiating from the front porch, not watching through the window.**
